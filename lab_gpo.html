Locking Down Control Panel with a Group Policy Object

Right after getting Remote Desktop working, I moved on to Group Policy — one of those AD tools that shows up in basically every IT job posting. The goal was simple on paper: stop a user from messing around in Control Panel. The actual point was proving to myself I could push a setting out to the domain and confirm it landed, not just assume it did.

Here's the scenario I had in mind: you don't want every random user in Control Panel changing system settings they shouldn't touch. Instead of walking to every machine one by one, you push a Group Policy Object out from the DC and it handles all of them at once. So I built one, applied it, and then actually went and checked that it worked on jsmith's account instead of just trusting it.

// Step 1: Make the GPO and link it

Opened Group Policy Management on DC01, right-clicked corp.local, and picked "Create a GPO in this domain, and Link it here..."

Right-clicking corp.local and creating a new GPO linked straight to the domain.

Called it Block Control Panel. Linking it at the domain level means it hits every user in corp.local — good enough for testing, though realistically I'd scope something like this to a specific OU instead of blasting the whole domain.

Block Control Panel now shows up under corp.local, right next to the Default Domain Policy.

// Step 2: Track down the actual setting

Opened the Group Policy Management Editor and dug into User Configuration → Policies → Administrative Templates → Control Panel.

Drilled down through User Configuration to the Control Panel node.
Five settings live here. The one I actually want: "Prohibit access to Control Panel and PC settings."

// Step 3: Flip it on

Opened that setting and set it to Enabled. Before clicking OK though, I actually read the Help panel on the right — it's easy to assume a setting like this does one narrow thing and it turns out to do way more.

Set to Enabled. Turns out this one blocks Control Panel from the Start screen, File Explorer, AND the right-click context menu — more than I expected from the name.
Glad I checked: I almost skipped reading the Help text, figured "prohibit access to control panel" was self-explanatory. Turns out it kills access from several different entry points at once, not just the obvious shortcut. Two extra seconds of reading, worth it.

// Step 4: Push it out right now

GPOs normally apply on their own schedule, but I wasn't about to sit around waiting for a refresh cycle. Ran gpupdate /force from PowerShell on DC01.

gpupdate /force — both Computer Policy and User Policy came back "completed successfully."

// Step 5: Actually prove it worked

Turning a setting on and knowing it actually applied are two different things — I wasn't going to just take Windows' word for it. Logged into CLIENT01 as jsmith and ran gpresult /r to see for myself.

There it is — "Applied Group Policy Objects: Block Control Panel." Landed on jsmith's session, pulled from DC01.corp.local.
Full RSOP dump for CORP\jsmith on CLIENT01 — confirms the policy applied at 3:33:47 PM from DC01.corp.local.
C:\Users\jsmith>gpresult /r

RSOP data for CORP\jsmith on CLIENT01 : Logging Mode
----------------------------------------------------

USER SETTINGS
--------------
Last time Group Policy was applied: 8/15/2026 at 3:33:47 PM
Group Policy was applied from: DC01.corp.local

Applied Group Policy Objects
-----------------------------
Block Control Panel

That was my confirmation — the GPO applied cleanly to jsmith, pulled from the right DC, nothing filtered it out. I could've gone and actually tried opening Control Panel as jsmith just to see it in action, but honestly gpresult already told me everything I needed to know.

// What I took away from this

  • Making a GPO and having it actually apply are two totally different milestones. gpupdate /force pushes it out — gpresult /r is how you prove it actually landed.
  • Read the Help text before you enable a policy. "Prohibit access to Control Panel" sounds narrow but it quietly locks down way more than just that one shortcut.
  • Linking at the domain level is quick for testing, but I know for a real environment I'd scope this down to a specific OU so it's not touching people it has no business touching.
  • gpresult /r is genuinely a great troubleshooting tool beyond just this — it also shows group membership and flags anything that got filtered out, which is exactly what I'd check first if someone said "the policy isn't working for me."
